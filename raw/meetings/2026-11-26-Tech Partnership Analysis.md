## 摘要

This presentation delivered by the representative of the University Alliance focuses on technology partnership scenarios between Europe and China, covering market differences, competitive advantages, cooperation cases, potential risks and organizational introduction, content as follows:
Sino-European Technology Market Differences
Technology Planning and Support Mechanism
China's top-down planning: China adopts a top-down technology promotion path, first setting up pilot zones for technology demonstration, then launching large-scale industrial rollout, with strong targeted financial incentives.
Europe's decentralized R&D model: The EU approves cross-national R&D projects such as Horizon Europe funds to connect universities and enterprises, but lacks direct top-down industrial promotion and sufficient financial incentives.
Regulatory Environment
China's relatively flexible regulation: For emerging sectors like autonomous driving, China has designated zones that allow technology implementation under a less restrictive regulatory framework.
Europe's strict regulatory system: Europe adopts a more regulation-driven governance model, with far stricter access rules for new technologies such as autonomous vehicles.


Sino-European Technology Market Mechanism Comparison

Typical Industry Development Reference Cases
Solar Industry Development Path
Early European R&D advantage: In the early 2000s, Europe (especially Germany) took the lead in solar technology R&D and incubated a large number of related startups.
China's large-scale industrialization: After China's solar industry scaled up rapidly for mass markets, all top 10 global solar equipment manufacturers are now Chinese, leading to the bankruptcy of many European solar companies in the 2010s.

Global Solar Industry Development Path
Electric Vehicle Industry Development Path
China's phased support: China launched EV support in 2009 via the "10 Cities, 1000 Vehicles" pilot, later implemented dual-credit policies, and the Tesla Shanghai plant further boosted the whole industry.
European late promotion: European OEMs did not push forward the EV technology they launched early, and the global EV export volume of Chinese-produced vehicles has surpassed Germany, Japan in recent years.
Four Core Competitive Pillars of China's Automotive Industry
Complete Supply Chain Scale and Availability: Within a 300-kilometer radius of the Yangtze River Delta, there is a complete supporting system for automotive production, which no 300-kilometer area in Europe can match.
Significant Cost Advantage: The price of battery packs in China is more than 56% lower than that in Europe, which comes from vertical integration, sufficient raw materials, lower factory construction and engineering costs, not just subsidies.
Leading Technology Iteration Level
Advanced battery technology layout: China has taken the lead in LFP, sodium-ion, semi-solid and solid-state battery technologies, and the gigacasting process has been widely adopted by local OEMs.
Convenient technology testing: China has a large number of pilot zones that support rapid technology testing, avoiding the lengthy regulatory approval process in Europe.
Extremely Fast Product Development Speed
Short development cycle: The average EV development cycle of Chinese startups is 20 months, far shorter than the 40-month standard in Europe, and the shortest can reach 18 months.
Early supplier involvement: Chinese OEMs bring suppliers into the project from the very beginning, skipping the long compliance-based supplier nomination process in Europe.
Flying Effect Formation: The scale advantage brings cost reduction, the cost advantage supports faster iteration, and the fast iteration further promotes technology optimization and larger scale expansion.

Four Core Competitive Pillars of China's Automotive Industry
Existing Sino-European Technology Cooperation Cases
Volkswagen and Horizon Robotics Cooperation: The cooperation has upgraded from the initial "China for China" localized R&D to the "China for the World" model, and the developed new platform will be promoted to global markets outside Europe.
Renault and WeRide Cooperation: Renault provides vehicle carriers, WeRide provides Level 4 autonomous driving software stack, and the third party is responsible for operation, which is still in the pilot stage.
Geely and Mercedes-Benz Smart Brand Cooperation: The R&D work is mainly completed in China, while the European partner is responsible for brand management, helping the brand achieve good performance in both Chinese and European small EV markets.
Schaeffler and Chinese Partner Humanoid Robot Cooperation: Schaeffler provides motion components and access to European and global markets, and the two sides jointly develop humanoid robot related technologies.
Global Layout Trend: Chinese OEMs have built about 24 overseas plants (including CKD assembly plants), and will build full production plants in Europe, which will bring more cooperation opportunities for local suppliers and research institutions.
Potential Risks for European Partners
Supply Chain Dependency Risk: Over-reliance on Chinese supply chains may face risks such as rare earth export control, which may force European enterprises to develop rare-earth-free motors.
Cost Fluctuation Risk: Additional tariffs that may be introduced later will push up the cost of Chinese supply chains, eroding the original cost advantage.
Product Safety Risk: Excessively shortened development cycles may lead to insufficient product verification, resulting in safety problems of EV products.
Intellectual Property Protection Risk: European partners worry that the core knowledge, processes and technologies they input in cooperation may be rapidly diffused without effective protection.
Future Cooperation Prospects
Level 4 autonomous driving: European OEMs can provide vehicle production capacity, while Chinese partners provide robotaxi related autonomous driving software.
Industrial robotics: European enterprises focus on developing software stacks, while Chinese partners are responsible for mass production of hardware to reduce overall costs.
Commercial EV trucks: European brands will cooperate with Chinese OEMs to maintain market competitiveness.
Alliance Introduction: The alliance connects German universities, Chinese and European enterprises and research institutions, to promote two-way technology partnership between China and Europe.

00:03  Technology Partnership Scenarios between Europe and China: Market Comparison & Analysis
This speech, presented on behalf of the University Alliance, focuses on Sino-European technology partnership scenarios. It compares the two regions' tech development models: China adopts a highly efficient top-down planning approach with pilot zones, strong industrial financial incentives and looser regulatory frameworks for new tech like autonomous vehicles, while EU carries out plans voted by 27 member states, funds university-company joint R&D via Horizon Europe projects with fewer financial incentives and far stricter regulations.
04:11  The Rise of China's Solar Industry and Its Impact on Early European Market Leaders
This session focuses on analyzing differences across corporate models, taking the solar industry as a typical example. The early R&D and technology of the solar industry were strongly concentrated in Europe, especially Germany, with a large number of related local startups. Later, China rapidly scaled up its solar industry and expanded mass production, leading most European solar companies to go bankrupt in the 2010s due to fierce competition. Now all the world's top 10 solar equipment manufacturers are Chinese, even with existing tariffs.
05:57  China's EV Industry Development and Its Rising Global R&D Influence
This section reviews global EV industry development: China launched EV support in 2009, European early EV tech was not fully pushed until Tesla drove progress, China’s supportive policies and 2019 Tesla Shanghai plant boosted the sector. China’s auto exports overtook Germany in 2022 and Japan in 2023, with 2024 total exports expected to hit 11 billion. A recent survey of German automakers in China shows the share of their local R&D applied globally rose from 12% in 2024 to 23% this year, proving China’s auto R&D now holds significant global weight.
09:33  Four Pillars Comparing China and Europe's Automotive (Especially EV) Sector Advantages
This chapter identifies four pillars for automotive sector comparison: scale & supply chain availability (China boasts dense 300km-radius EV supply chains far beyond Europe), cost (56%+ battery pack price gap driven by vertical integration, accessible raw materials, lower factory investment and construction speed), advanced auto-related technologies, and far faster development cycles (18-20 months in China vs 40 months in Europe). These advantages form a mutually reinforcing "flying effect" that will also support China's leading position in robotics.
17:58  Volkswagen's Evolution from "China for China" to "China for the World" Strategy
This section cites Volkswagen as a typical corporation case: it launched the "China for China" strategy about 5 years ago, the speaker once discussed related matters with its CTO back then. Recently Volkswagen released its new platform, shifting its strategy to "China and beyond" which means "China for the world". It plans to promote its China-developed products and relevant platforms to global markets (excluding Europe at the initial stage), leveraging China's supply chain, development speed and technology access to realize this transformation.
19:11  A Technology Partnership Pilot Case Between Renault and Vira for Level 4 Shuttles in Paris
This chapter introduces a representative Level 4 autonomous driving shuttle pilot partnership case in Paris. The ongoing pilot program is jointly carried out by three parties: Renault provides the vehicles, Vira offers the Level 4 autonomous driving technical stack, and Betty takes charge of the relevant operation work. The speaker regards this multi-party technology collaboration mode as a highly valuable reference example for future industry practice.
19:53  Competency-complementary Sino-European Cooperation Driving Smart Brand's Success in Small EV Segment
This section focuses on the G Messenger's cooperation on the smart auto brand, which is a key influential brand in Europe especially in the small vehicle segment. Many large European players quitted the small EV sector due to low profitability, while the smart brand sees great success both in China and Europe with new upcoming models, its product development is mainly based in China and brand management is handled by its European partner.
20:55  Cross-Regional Collaboration Trends Between Global Automotive Players and Related Cooperation Opportunities
This chapter cites a chef-robot collaboration case demonstrating cross-regional two-company cooperation, notes nearly 24 overseas Chinese auto-related factories (many Chinese OEMs now build formal not merely CKD assembly plants in Europe), points out this trend brings abundant cooperation opportunities for Chinese suppliers, testing research institutes and local European enterprises, which is the reverse of past situation where European OEMs set up R&D centers and JVs in China.
23:08  Potential Risks Related to China for European Auto Industry Development
This section sorts out the risks European parties perceive for China-related supply chain and EV industry development. The risks include rare earth export control-related supply chain dependency, potential upcoming higher tariffs that may undermine the cost advantage and operation speed of relevant supply chains, insufficient vehicle validation brought by excessive pursuit of development speed leading to EV safety hazards, and concerns on intellectual property protection during Sino-foreign cooperation.
25:18  Key Cooperation Perspectives on Autonomous Driving and Robotics Between China and Europe
This section concludes the presentation by outlining key cross-regional collaboration trends, noting that European firms, especially in Level 4 autonomous driving and robotics, tend to focus on software stacks rather than hardware, while Chinese partners provide vehicle/robot hardware. It emphasizes that cost control for robotics scaling is critical to avoid a race to the bottom for enterprise survival, and such mutually viable partnerships including in EV commercial vehicle fields are already ongoing and will grow more prevalent.
27:46  Overview of the Sino-European Innovation and Business Alliance
This section sees the speaker finish a brief overview of their organization, an innovation and business alliance that facilitates two-way cooperation: it connects German university partners, enterprises like FDB and other German entities with Chinese parties, and links Chinese young talents, universities and other institutes with Europe. It is also in talks with European R&E locations to promote tech partnerships between the two regions. The speaker wraps up the presentation, looks forward to follow-up discussions during the break and the subsequent presentations.
